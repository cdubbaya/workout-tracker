# Recovery is the user's call, not the program's

The vision doc required rest days built into the prescribed schedule. We are removing them. The program sets a Session Goal each day; the user decides how much of it to do. A light day is whatever the user says it is, and it pays Attendance XP plus the XP of whatever reps they managed.

This is a deliberate trade of a safety rail for a better daily experience. The alternative on the table was autoregulation — dropping tomorrow's Session Goal after several consecutive full-effort days, which would have bound real load because the Session Cap *is* the Session Goal. We chose not to.

## The risk we are accepting

The vision doc lists volume injury as a live risk and made scheduled rest its mitigation. Programs schedule rest precisely because motivated users do not choose it, and this product now stacks a daily Streak, friend challenges, and social visibility on top of a user who owns their own recovery. Nothing in the system lowers the dose.

What remains is incentive-level rather than load-level, and the distinction matters:

- The Session Cap and Daily Cap remove the *reward* for extra volume. They do not prevent it. A user can still do three hundred push-ups; the app simply won't pay for them.
- Holding the Streak costs one Counted Rep, so the streak never demands a hard day.
- Attendance XP means a bad day still pays, so quitting early is not a loss.

Together those remove most of the reasons a rational user would overreach. They do nothing about an irrational one, and this product's audience includes beginners in the middle of a friend challenge.

## Consequences

- The medical disclaimer the vision doc requires at onboarding is now load-bearing rather than ceremonial, and it should say that the user manages their own recovery.
- Free-form logging is the sharpest edge, since it sits outside even the Session Goal. The Daily Cap is the only thing watching it.
- Revisit if the instrumentation shows users sustaining full-goal sessions for long unbroken runs, or if challenge participation correlates with dropoff that looks like injury rather than boredom. Autoregulation remains implementable later without disturbing anything else — it changes one number.
